Step 1 — See what's actually on your reports

Your credit score is calculated from your credit reports at the three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. You're entitled to pull all three free at AnnualCreditReport.com, the federally authorized site. Read them line by line and mark anything you don't recognize: accounts that aren't yours, late payments that weren't late, balances that are wrong, debts listed twice.

It also helps to know what the score is made of. FICO publishes the five factors and their weights:

Payment history — 35%Paying on time is the single biggest lever.
Amounts owed — 30%How much of your available credit you're using.
Length of history — 15%Older accounts help; think twice before closing them.
Credit mix — 10%A blend of cards, loans, and other accounts.
New credit — 10%Many applications in a short window can drag you down.

Step 2 — Dispute what's wrong, in writing

Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, the bureaus have to investigate items you dispute and remove anything they can't verify. Three kinds of letters do most of the work — these are the same three the CreditFix Kit includes, ready to personalize:

  • Bureau error disputes — for accounts, balances, or late marks that are simply incorrect.
  • Collector validation requests — a debt collector has to prove the debt is yours and that they may collect it before it belongs on your report.
  • Goodwill adjustments — a polite, specific ask to a creditor you otherwise pay on time to remove a one-off late mark.

Send disputes in writing, keep copies of everything, and give the bureau its investigation window before following up. Then repeat for each bureau — an error fixed at one often still sits on the other two.

Step 3 — Rebuild month by month

Disputes clean the past; habits build the future. The CreditFix Kit's twelve-month plan sequences the boring things that actually move a score:

  • Autopay at least the minimum on every account, so a new late mark never lands.
  • Pay cards down early in the cycle so the balance reported to the bureaus is lower.
  • Ask for credit-limit increases on cards you already have instead of opening new ones.
  • Add one small, on-time payment stream — a credit-builder account — and let it age.

Step 4 — Get a card that builds, not one that traps

Once your reports are clean and a few on-time months are behind you, a card you actually qualify for becomes a tool: small purchases, paid in full, reported every month. Be wary of cards aimed at low scores that bury you in sign-up and monthly fees before you've spent a dollar — read the fee schedule first, every time.
